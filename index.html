CHILDREN & TEENS

The child who says “I don’t care.”

Sometimes the words that sound like indifference are asking a much harder question: Is it safe to care out loud?

Read the story ↗

“I don’t care.” Three words that can bring a conversation to a stop. For a parent, they may sound dismissive. For a young person, they can sometimes feel like armor.

Not every shrug hides pain. Sometimes a child genuinely isn't interested. But when “I don’t care” appears right after disappointment, embarrassment, or conflict, curiosity may take us further than correction.

Instead of demanding an explanation in the moment, try: “You don’t have to talk about it right now. I’m here when you want to.” Then make room for the conversation to happen later, without an audience or a deadline.

Connection doesn’t mean abandoning boundaries. It means remembering that the person behind the behavior still needs to feel seen.

RELATIONSHIPS

When the arguments stop, but the distance grows.

Silence can look like peace from the outside. Inside a relationship, it can tell a different story.

Read the story ↗

People often think the worst part of a struggling relationship is the fighting. Sometimes it’s what happens when two people stop trying to be understood.

The house may be quieter. The daily routines may continue. But the small invitations disappear: a story from the day, a hand on the shoulder, the question “What’s really going on with you?”

Quiet doesn’t always mean disconnection, and conflict isn’t proof that a relationship is failing. What matters is whether both people still have room to be honest and feel safe doing so.

One place to begin is small: “I miss feeling close to you. Could we talk for ten minutes without trying to solve everything?” Rebuilding connection is rarely one grand conversation. More often, it’s the decision to keep showing up for the next one.

PERSONAL GROWTH

You can love someone and still need boundaries.

Caring deeply about another person doesn’t require disappearing from your own life.

Read the story ↗

For some of us, saying no feels like letting someone down. We say yes because we love them, because they need us, or because conflict feels harder than exhaustion.

But a boundary isn’t a punishment. It’s a way to be honest about what you can offer without building resentment around what you cannot.

It might sound like: “I care about you, and I can listen tonight for twenty minutes. After that, I need to rest.” The words can be gentle and still be firm.

Healthy boundaries take practice. The goal isn’t to stop caring. It’s to make room for care that is sustainable for both people.
